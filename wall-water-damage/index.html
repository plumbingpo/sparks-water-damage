Wall and Drywall Water Damage: What Sparks Homeowners Should Know

A sheet of drywall behaves much as a wick does. Paper and gypsum pull water up from the floor line, so the wet zone frequently ends a foot or more past the highest point the water itself touched. The painted face can look and feel normal while the back side and the insulation behind it are soaked. For that reason our team relies on moisture meter readings rather than appearance.

Plenty of walls that get wet can stay. When the water was clean and the drywall remains firm, drying it where it stands is often possible, at times by pulling the baseboard and pushing air into the cavity through small holes. Walls that are mushy, falling apart, moldy or contaminated get a flood cut instead, where our crew cuts straight across above the damage and takes out whatever sits under that line.

What It Looks Like

  • One wall noticeably cooler under your hand than the rest
  • Soft or crumbly spots you notice when pushing lightly on the wall
  • Trim along the floor that has puffed up, twisted or come loose
  • Flooring along the wall that is damp, stained or starting to lift
  • Nail or screw heads popping out and seams showing through the paint
  • New gaps along casings and baseboard tops as swollen material shifts
Our Process

What Happens After You Call

1

Find Every Wet Area

Using meter readings and thermal images, our team outlines where the water went, upward and sideways, on both faces of the wall and in neighboring rooms.

2

Sort Save From Remove

You get a straight answer on what can be dried where it is and what must be removed, judged by the category of water, the time it sat and the firmness of the drywall.

3

Cut or Ventilate

For walls being saved, baseboards come off and air is directed into the cavity. On sections coming out, a straight flood cut is made and soaked insulation is removed.

4

Dry and Rebuild

Drying typically takes 3 to 5 days of air movement and dehumidification before the framing and surviving drywall meter dry. After that comes insulation, new drywall hung and finished, trim put back and paint.

What You Cannot See

The Damage You Do Not See

Electrical Hazards Low Down

Water pools at the same height as most receptacles and cable runs. Until that circuit is inspected, avoid plugging anything into the affected wall.

Water Next Door

It slips beneath the bottom plate and reaches the closet or room on the other side. Every wall involved is tested on its two faces, and so is the adjacent flooring.

Soaked Batts in the Wall

Batts of fiberglass absorb water and keep it pressed to the framing. Drying from inside the room is not enough when the insulation is soaked.

Mold Inside Cavities

The back of the drywall is dark, still and lined with paper, which is ideal for mold. You can have a clean looking painted surface with heavy growth right behind it.

The Clock

The Damage Timeline

The first few hours

Water Starts Climbing

Moisture enters where the panel meets the floor and wicks upward from there. Both the baseboard and the bottom plate start absorbing water.

24 to 48 hours

Swelling and Blisters

The wet line rises higher, baseboards swell and paint begins to blister. Growth is able to get going on the paper that lines the hidden side.

Day 3 to 7

Drywall Breaks Down

Gypsum softens and the paper lets go. Insulation slumps inside the wall, odor builds and mold spreads across the hidden back side.

After a week

Damage Reaches the Studs

Studs and plates stay wet long enough to grow mold and begin to decay. The amount of removal and reconstruction grows considerably.
